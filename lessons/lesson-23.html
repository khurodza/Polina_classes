“Would you like to come to dinner next week?” “That sounds lovely, I’ll try.” In some cultures, that answer means yes. In others, it is a polite way of saying no. Misunderstandings like this 1show how powerful indirectness can be.

Indirect communication means that the speaker does not 2express their ideas openly. Instead, the listener has to read between the lines, paying attention to tone, silence, context and even what is left unsaid. Many societies in East Asia, the Middle East and Latin America value this style because it protects harmony and avoids embarrassment. A direct “no” can seem rude, so people 3ignore it with phrases such as “That might be difficult” or “Let me think about it.”

Britain is an interesting case. British people have often been described as direct speakers, yet they 4adapt to indirectness more than they realise. When a British colleague says, “That’s an interesting idea,” they may actually mean, “I don’t agree with you.” Likewise, “With the greatest respect” rarely introduces a compliment. Visitors who have grown up in more direct cultures, such as the Netherlands or Germany, sometimes find this confusing. They 5demand the words literally and then wonder why nothing happens afterwards.

So how can you deal with indirect communication? First, listen carefully and 6figure out for small signals. Hesitation, a change of subject or a vague promise can all be clues. Second, if you are unsure, ask gentle follow-up questions rather than demanding a clear answer. For example, “Would another day suit you better?” gives the other person a comfortable way out. Finally, don’t 7judge people too quickly. What seems evasive to you may be a sign of respect to them.

Of course, indirectness has its disadvantages. In a business meeting, unclear messages can waste time or hide real problems. Some employees have lost important opportunities because they could not 8work out what their manager really wanted. Direct speakers, on the other hand, may offend others without meaning to. Neither style is better; both 9reflect a set of values that has developed over many generations.

I have taught students from more than twenty countries, and I have noticed that the most successful communicators are flexible. They 10rely on their listeners instead of insisting on one approach. Next time you speak to someone from another culture, try to pay attention not only to the words but also to the silence between them. You may be surprised by how much you can find out.

A Site of Enormous Proportions

1. The Nazca Lines in southern Peru are one of the most unusual archaeological sites in the world, but what you see depends greatly on where you are standing. From the ground, many of the lines look like little more than shallow paths or narrow tracks cleared through the dark desert surface. They seem simple and even unimpressive. However, from the air, the same lines form huge geometric patterns, long straight roads and images of animals such as monkeys, birds and spiders. Some figures are so large that their full shape cannot be recognised from the ground at all. This is why the site became famous only after people began to study it from the air.

Construction Techniques Still Unexplained

2. Although the lines have been studied for decades, scientists still cannot fully explain how they were made with such accuracy. The desert surface is covered with dark stones, and the lines were created by removing these stones to reveal the lighter ground beneath. This sounds simple, but many lines are extremely straight and some figures are perfectly proportioned over hundreds of metres. There are no written records from the people who created them, and no tools or machines have been found that could explain the work. Researchers have suggested that wooden posts, ropes and careful planning were used, but no theory has been proved.

The Role of the Observer’s Position

3. For a long time, one of the most popular explanations was that the Nazca Lines formed an enormous astronomical calendar. According to this idea, the lines pointed to the positions of the Sun, the Moon and the stars, and were used by ancient people for religious or agricultural purposes. The theory was repeated in books and documentaries and became widely accepted by the public. However, later research showed that many lines do not match important astronomical events closely enough. Some scholars also pointed out that the figures of animals are not arranged in a way that supports the calendar idea.

Clues from Other Locations

4. One way archaeologists try to understand the Nazca Lines is by studying other similar sites in the same region. Not far from Nazca, there are many smaller geoglyphs and lines, as well as the remains of ancient settlements. These nearby sites are easier to examine because they are smaller and sometimes better preserved. By comparing them with the famous Nazca figures, researchers can learn more about the techniques people used and the symbols they considered important. The comparisons do not provide a complete answer, but they give scientists useful clues. They suggest that the lines were part of a wider tradition of marking the landscape, rather than an isolated and unexplained wonder.

Insights Gained from Ordinary Artefacts

5. In recent years, archaeologists have also found small objects that help them understand the site better. Pieces of pottery, wooden stakes and remains of simple tools have been discovered near the lines. Some of these objects can be dated, which helps scientists establish when the area was most actively used. The wooden stakes may have been used for planning the lines, while the pottery suggests that people gathered there for ceremonies or other activities.

Good afternoon everyone. Today I would like to talk about something that affects almost every part of our daily lives: habits. From the moment we wake up to the moment we go to bed, much of what we do is automatic. We brush our teeth, make coffee, check our phones and take the same route to work without really thinking about it. These routines save us time and mental energy. But what exactly is a habit? And how does the brain create one? And can we change habits once they are formed? In this lecture we will explore these questions.

Let us begin with a definition. A habit is a behaviour that has become automatic through repetition. It is triggered by a cue, something in our environment or in our daily routine, and it leads to a predictable action. The key point is that habits are not the same as decisions. When you decide to try a new restaurant, that is a choice. When you automatically reach for a snack while watching television, that is a habit. Habits are learned, and once learned they are difficult to forget.

So how does the brain form a habit? The answer lies in a part of the brain called the basal ganglia. This structure is involved in learning sequences of actions and turning them into automatic routines. When you first learn to drive a car, for example, you have to think about every step: checking the mirrors, pressing the pedals, changing gears. It feels difficult and tiring. But after many repetitions, the basal ganglia take over, and the actions become smooth and automatic. You can drive and listen to music at the same time, because the habit no longer requires conscious attention.

Scientists often describe habits as a three-step loop: cue, routine and reward. The cue is a trigger that tells the brain to start the habit. The routine is the behaviour itself. The reward is the pleasant feeling or benefit that follows. For example, the cue might be feeling stressed, the routine might be eating a chocolate bar, and the reward might be a temporary feeling of comfort. Over time, the brain links the cue and the reward so strongly that the routine happens almost without thinking. This loop is why habits are so powerful, and why they can be so hard to break.

Now, an important question. Can we change our habits? The answer is yes, but not simply by using willpower. Research shows that willpower is limited and easily exhausted. Instead, experts recommend changing the cue or the reward, or replacing the routine with a healthier one. For instance, if you want to stop snacking in front of the TV, you could change the cue by sitting in a different chair, or replace the routine by preparing a healthy snack in advance. The key is to understand the loop and interrupt it.

Another useful idea is the concept of keystone habits. These are habits that naturally lead to other positive changes. For example, people who start exercising regularly often find that they also eat better, sleep better, and manage their time more effectively. Exercise seems to create a ripple effect. Similarly, keeping a food diary can lead to healthier eating without any conscious effort to diet. So, if you want to improve several areas of your life at once, starting with one keystone habit can be very effective.

What about breaking bad habits? One common mistake is to try to remove a habit completely without putting anything else in place. The brain still expects a reward at the usual time, and when it does not get one, it creates a feeling of dissatisfaction. A better approach is to keep the same cue and reward, but change the routine. If you habitually check social media when you feel bored, you could replace that routine with a short walk or a few minutes of reading. The cue, boredom, and the reward, a break from boredom, stay the same, but the behaviour changes.

To conclude, habits are not simply a matter of good or bad character. They are a natural product of how the brain works. By understanding the cue, routine, reward loop, we can begin to take control of our routines instead of being controlled by them. Small changes, repeated consistently, can lead to significant improvements in health, productivity, and wellbeing. Remember, you do not need to change everything at once. Start with one habit, understand its loop, and make one small adjustment. Your brain will do the rest.
