4 · How to read the result

The paper is a little harder than the demo in Section 1 and about the same elsewhere. Compare the four section bars with her Lesson 9 result, not only the total. What each loss usually means: points lost in Задание 1 on gaps 3, 6 or 10 are about time markers (Lesson 11), on 4–6 about the passive (Lesson 14). In Задание 2, a wrong word of the right form is vocabulary (decks V10, V11); a word of the wrong form is the routine. In Задание 5, look at where the chain broke: a break at B/H is time order, at D/K it is a pronoun. In listening, check whether she fell for nearly (twice in Задание 7) and for the number in statement 2. In Задание 9 every lost answer is a V14 card; in Задание 10 it is a date on the timeline.

The page also records how long she took. If she finished with more than eight minutes to spare and lost points in Section 1, the advice for the round is to use the spare time on Задания 1 and 4 (spelling, the, plural), not to hand in early.

🔑 Teacher only · Key, Задание 1
  1. broke: a date, a finished event → past simple
  2. were sleeping: at that moment → past continuous (background)
  3. had fallen: since the previous autumn inside a past story → past perfect
  4. were built (also had been built): houses are built → passive, plural

  6. had been destroyed: by the time + past → past perfect passive

🔎 Why · the marker decides the form
  • 1 broke: a finished event with a date (2 September 1666) → past simple.
  • 2 were sleeping: at that moment: the background, in progress when the fire began → past continuous.
  • 3 had fallen: since the previous autumn, inside a past story: earlier than the fire → past perfect.
  • 4 were built: houses don’t build themselves → passive; houses is plural → were.
  • 5 be stopped: after a modal (could not) the passive is be + the third form.
  • 6 had been destroyed: by the time + past → past perfect; the houses did not act → passive.
  • 7 leaving: a comma and no new subject: an -ing clause that gives the result.
  • 8 the worst: that London had ever seen → superlative, and a superlative needs the.
  • 9 designed: between two commas, a passive meaning (it was designed by Wren) → an -ed clause.
  • 10 has been climbed: since it opened up to now → present perfect; by millions → passive.

Found only in New Zealand, it is the heaviest parrot in the world, weighing up to four kilograms, and the only one that cannot fly. It is active at night, climbs trees with its claws and beak, and may live for ninety years, longer than almost any other bird. In nearly every way, this species stands out from the rest of its colourful, noisy family.
2
For millions of years there were no land mammals in New Zealand apart from bats, and the kakapo’s enemies were birds of prey, such as a giant eagle, that hunted by sight. The parrot adapted perfectly: its green feathers matched the forest floor, and whenever it sensed danger it simply stood still. Then people arrived, bringing rats, cats, dogs and stoats with them. These predators hunt with their noses, not their eyes, so a bird that freezes and smells of honey is the easiest prey imaginable. What had protected the species for so long now made it helpless.
3
By the 1970s many scientists believed that the kakapo had died out. Then a small group was discovered on Stewart Island, in the far south, but cats were killing those birds too. The government took a bold decision. Between 1980 and 1997 every bird that could be caught was carried, one by one, to a few small islands off the coast from which all predators had been removed. By 1997 the last birds had been taken off Stewart Island. It was a huge risk, yet without it the species would almost certainly have been wiped out.
4
Safety did not solve everything, because kakapo breed extremely slowly. Females lay eggs only in the years when the rimu, a tall native conifer, produces a heavy crop of fruit, which happens once every two to four years. The chicks are fed almost entirely on that fruit. In the other years, almost no eggs are laid. Scientists have tried offering the birds extra food, but nothing they have come up with has persuaded them to nest more often. The future of the whole species therefore rises and falls with the harvest of one kind of plant.
5
Today each kakapo has a name and wears a tiny radio transmitter that tells rangers where it is, whether it has mated and whether it is sitting on a nest. Eggs at risk are hatched in incubators, and the DNA of every living bird has been read in order to keep the fragile population healthy. Drones have even been used to carry samples from one island to another. Thanks to these methods the population has grown from 51 birds in 1995 to about 250. That is real progress, but the kakapo remains one of the most vulnerable birds on Earth.

Good morning, everybody. Since most of you have exams in a few weeks, I thought I’d talk today about forgetting.

The first person to study it scientifically was a German psychologist, Hermann Ebbinghaus, in the 1880s. He had no laboratory and no volunteers, so he conducted every experiment on himself.1 False He made up more than two thousand meaningless syllables, things like zof and wid, learned lists of them by heart, and then tested himself after twenty minutes, after an hour, after a day, a week and a month. He carried on like this for years.

The outcome was what we now call the forgetting curve. Ebbinghaus found that memory doesn’t fade at a steady speed. After about an hour, he had already lost more than half of what he had learned.2 False After a day, about two thirds had gone.trap for 2 But after that, the line became almost flat: what he still knew after a week, he mostly still knew after a month. In other words, forgetting is fastest at the very beginning.

Now, you might think that results from one man and his notebook, nearly a century and a half ago, can’t be very accurate. But in 2015, two researchers in Amsterdam repeated the whole experiment, with the same kind of syllables and the same timetable, and the curve they got was almost identical to his.3 True

So what can we do about it? Ebbinghaus himself figured out the first answer. Every time he went back to a list, he forgot it more slowly afterwards. So it’s far better to revise something for ten minutes on five different days than for fifty minutes on one evening.

The second answer is more recent. In 2006, two American psychologists gave students a short text to study. Half of the students read it four times. The other half read it once and then, three times, tried to write down everything they could remember, without looking. Straight afterwards, the readers felt much more confident, and they predicted that they would remember more.4 False A week later, it turned out to be the other way roundtrap for 4: the students who had tested themselves remembered about sixty per cent of the text, and the readers only forty.

And that’s the trap. Reading your notes again feels like learning, because the page looks familiar. But recognising something isn’t the same as being able to produce it. Closing the book and trying to recall what was in it feels harder and less pleasant, and that’s precisely why it works.5 True

So, my advice. Don’t rely on one long night with your notes before the exam. Start early, come back to each topic several times, and test yourself every time. And then go to bed, because sleep is when the brain decides what to keep.
