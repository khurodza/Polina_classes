Task 1 — The Organising Principle

🎨 Learn them by colour, not alphabetically.

The olympiad corpus builds whole tasks on one colour, so learn the idioms by colour, not alphabetically. Red = anger, danger, debt and bureaucracy. Blue = aristocracy, sadness, the police. Black = formality and disgrace. White = surrender and uselessness. That mapping makes fifteen items behave like four.

Task 2 — Guess

🤔 What do you think these mean?

Use the colour map above. Say your guess out loud — then open the answer.

black tie
black = formality
Reveal
Very formal evening dress — a dinner jacket for men, a long dress for women. Seen on invitations: Dress code: black tie.
feeling blue
blue = sadness
Reveal
Feeling sad or low, usually for no big reason. She's been feeling blue since her best friend moved away. The noun is the blues.
in the red
red = debt
Reveal
Owing money; in debt — from the red ink accountants used for losses. The club has been in the red for two seasons. Opposite: in the black.
a white flag
white = surrender
Reveal
A sign that you give up — from the battlefield signal for surrender. After an hour of arguing, Dad raised the white flag and let us stay up.

Oscar Wilde (1854–1900) — Irish wit and playwright, famous for epigrams; also The Canterville Ghost, Lady Windermere's Fan.

Virginia Woolf (1882–1941) — modernist, stream of consciousness, central figure of the Bloomsbury Group; also Orlando, A Room of One's Own.

The Canterville Ghost (1887). A brash American family buys an English country house and refuses to be frightened by its resident ghost.

Task 1 — The Patterns

🎵 Four intonation patterns.

The voice moves on the highlighted word. Read every example aloud and exaggerate the movement the first time — then do it naturally.

↘word voice goes down here ↗word voice goes up here
↘
Falling
statementscommandsWh- questions

The default. The voice drops on the last important word. It sounds calm, neutral and finished — you'll hear it at the end of most sentences in everyday talk.

💬 Statements
  • I like your ↘haircut.
  • We moved to New ↘York.
  • They have three ↘cats.
  • He was born in ↘September.
❗ Commands
  • Go to your ↘room.
  • Bring me my ↘keys.
  • Open the ↘door.
  • Help me carry this ↘upstairs.
❓ Wh- questions
  • Who is that ↘man?
  • What is your ↘name?
  • When will you be ↘home?
  • Where are you ↘going?
  • Why does he look ↘sad?
  • How are you ↘feeling?
↗
Rising
yes/no questionstag questions

“Your turn.” The voice goes up at the end and stays hanging — it invites the other person to answer.

🙋 Yes/No questions
  • Are you a ↗Gemini?
  • Do you want to watch a ↗film?
  • Is he your ↗friend?
  • May I use the ↗bathroom?
🏷️ Tag questions
  • We studied English together, ↗didn’t we?
  • You want to go to the park, ↗don’t you?
  • The weather is so nice, ↗isn’t it?
  • He likes bowling, ↗doesn’t he?
→
Flat
“monotone”avoid it

The robot voice. The pitch doesn't move at all. English speakers don't do this in real conversation — it sounds bored or mechanical. If you're told you sound flat, make your voice move.

↗↘
Rise-fall
either / orlistsif-sentences

Up, then down. The rise says “there's more coming”; the fall says “and that's all.” Two arrows, one sentence.

⚖️ Either / or
  • You can have either ↗the fish or ↘the beef.
  • Do you want to go ↗hiking or ↘fishing?
  • Is she ↗a student or ↘a teacher?
  • Is the football game on ↗Saturday or ↘Sunday?
📋 Lists
  • I have ↗three books, two pencils, one ruler, and ↘one eraser.
  • I like ↗swimming, dancing, and ↘playing tennis.
  • The apartment includes ↗two bedrooms, two bathrooms, and ↘a kitchen.
  • He ate ↗breakfast, lunch, and ↘dinner.
🔀 Conditionals
  • If you have any ↗questions, feel free to give me a ↘call.
  • If you keep having ↗pain, put ice on ↘it.
  • If you’re ↗happy, I’m ↘happy.
  • If he wants to ↗come, he needs to start ↘packing.

Sport in the US 2

The world would be a sadder place for many people if basketball (a) (not exist). If you do some research into the origins of the sport, you (b) (see) that it was the invention of one man. That's right. If it (c) (not be) for James Naismith (1861–1939), basketball would never have existed. Naismith was a Canadian PE teacher who worked in the US. Naismith's original baskets were wooden fruit baskets which didn't have a hole. If we (g) (have) the same baskets today, we would need a ladder to get the ball back every time a player scored! If basketball hadn't become so popular, maybe people (h) (forget) about James Naismith a long time ago. But it's thanks to him that one of the world's most popular sports exists.
