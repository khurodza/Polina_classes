“Wonderful things”

In November 1922, the British archaeologist Howard Carter was working in the Valley of the Kings in Egypt. He had been there for five years, but he had found almost nothing. Lord Carnarvon, who was paying for the dig, had decided that this would be the last season.

On 4 November, while the workers were clearing sand, a boy found a stone step. The team dug down, and three weeks later they reached a sealed door. Carter made a small hole and looked inside with a candle. When Carnarvon asked if he could see anything, Carter answered: “Yes, wonderful things.” It was the tomb of Tutankhamun.

📜 Script

Good afternoon, and welcome to the Sutton Hoo gallery. In the summer of 1939, just weeks before the Second World War broke out, a local archaeologist called Basil Brown began digging in a field in Suffolk. The land belonged to a woman named Edith Pretty, who had always been curious about the large mounds on her estate.

What Brown found was extraordinary: the outline of a ship, twenty-seven metres long. The wood had rotted away long ago, but the iron rivets remained in the sand, so the shape of the ship could still be seen.

In the middle of the ship was a burial chamber full of treasure: gold buckles, silver bowls from the Eastern Roman Empire, and a famous iron helmet. No body was found, which puzzled experts for years. Today, most historians believe that the ship was the grave of Raedwald, a king of East Anglia who died around the year 624.

After the dig, Edith Pretty generously gave the whole treasure to the nation, and it is now on display in the British Museum in London. The helmet you can see in front of you is a copy. The original was found broken into more than five hundred pieces, and it took experts years to put it back together.

💡Hint · the True / False traps
  • Before / after: “weeks before the war” makes “after the war had started” False.
  • Certain vs believe: “most historians believe” ≠ “historians are certain”.
  • Copy vs original: listen for the one word that changes everything.

💡Hint · relative clauses in one minute

A relative clause adds information about a noun, so two short sentences become one longer one:

  • who for people: Sargon was a king who conquered the Sumerian cities.
  • which / that for things: They built ziggurats, which were temples shaped like stepped pyramids.
  • where for places: Mesopotamia is the region where the first cities appeared.
  • whose for possession: Gilgamesh was a king whose story was written on clay tablets.

Mix in the other grammar: The cities were built out of mud (passive) · By 1750 BCE the Sumerians had disappeared (past perfect) · While scribes were studying, … (past continuous).
