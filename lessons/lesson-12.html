Hook · 1 min

Vikings in Novgorod, Vikings in England

The chronicle says that in 862 a Viking called Rurik came to rule Novgorod. In 865 a huge army landed in England. Who do you think they were, and where did they come from?

💡Answer · open after you’ve guessed

They were Vikings too, from Scandinavia. Swedish Vikings (in Russian, варяги) went east along the rivers to Rus’; Danish and Norwegian Vikings went west to Britain. The same people shaped both countries in the same century.

Before 1066: Romans, Saxons and Vikings

Five thousand years ago, long before anyone wrote history down, farmers in the south of Britain began building Stonehenge. Britain enters written history much later, in 55 BC, when Julius Caesar crossed the Channel. He came twice, but he did not stay.

The real Roman invasion began in AD 43. Within forty years Rome had conquered most of what is now England and Wales. Not everyone accepted it. In AD 60 Boudica, queen of the Iceni, led a revolt and burnt London, but the Romans defeated her. In AD 122 Emperor Hadrian ordered a wall across the north, to keep out the tribes of Scotland. Its remains still stand today.

As the empire went into decline, the Romans left. By 410 Britain had to defend itself. Angles, Saxons and Jutes sailed in from Germany and Denmark and set up their own kingdoms. The word England has its origin in their name: the land of the Angles. In 597 a monk called Augustine brought Christianity to Kent, and in 731 Bede, a monk in the north, finished the first history of the English people.

Then came the Vikings. In 793 they attacked the monastery of Lindisfarne, and by the 870s they had taken over much of the north and east. Only Wessex survived, under King Alfred the Great, who stopped the Vikings and made peace with them. His grandson Athelstan united the kingdoms and became the first King of all England in 927. But the Vikings were not finished. In 1016 the Danish king Cnut took the English throne.

The last Anglo-Saxon king, Edward the Confessor, died in January 1066 without children. Three men wanted his crown. Harold became king and defeated a Norwegian army at Stamford Bridge, but three days later William, Duke of Normandy, landed in the south. On 14 October 1066, at the Battle of Hastings, Harold was killed. William the Conqueror was crowned on Christmas Day, and the Viking age in England was over.

🔗Why this order
  • 1 = K: opener: the full name and who she was
  • 2 = E: “That first ten-minute ride” = the flight in 1 (that + noun)
  • 3 = D: “however”: she had to learn, but lessons were expensive
  • 4 = B: “she had saved enough”: the money from those jobs (past perfect = before buying)
  • 5 = G: a new stage, with a date; “only as a passenger” prepares 6
  • 6 = L: “to do it on her own” = to cross the Atlantic herself, not as a passenger
  • 7 = H: “this goal” = the solo flight in 6 (this + noun)
  • 8 = C: “The flight” = the one in 7 (the + noun again)
  • 9 = I: “Five years later” counts from 1932
  • 10 = J: “Tragically … has never been found”: the end of the story

The extras: A (“These Sunday…”) F (“The second…”). Both point to things the text never mentions (Sunday lessons with a brother, a first attempt).
