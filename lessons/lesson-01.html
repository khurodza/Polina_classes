Grammar Rule
📐 The Inward–Outward Principle
TAKE ←

brings something toward you or removes it

  • take a risk
  • take medicine
  • take a photograph
  • take control
  • take someone's temperature
GIVE →

sends something outward — often a sound or a gesture

  • give a smile
  • give a laugh
  • give a sigh
  • give a shout
  • give a speech

📖 These people are all part of English-language culture. Read about them and be ready to discuss.
Computing
Ada Lovelace
Daughter of the poet Lord Byron, she collaborated with Charles Babbage on his Analytical Engine and wrote what is considered the world's first computer algorithm (1843). Ada Lovelace Day is celebrated each October to honour women in STEM.
Computing
Charles Babbage
Designed the Difference Engine (1822) to calculate mathematical tables without human error, and later the Analytical Engine — the first concept of a general-purpose programmable computer. Neither machine was completed in his lifetime. He also invented an early version of the cowcatcher fitted to the front of trains.
Computing
Alan Turing
Studied at King's College, Cambridge, and invented the theoretical 'Turing Machine' — the foundation of modern computer science. During WWII he led the team at Bletchley Park that cracked the German naval Enigma code, saving countless lives. His 1950 paper introduced the Turing Test for machine intelligence. He was prosecuted for homosexuality in 1952 and given a royal pardon in 2013. His portrait appears on the British £50 note.
Music
Leonard Bernstein
Conductor, composer and pianist. In 1958 he became Music Director of the New York Philharmonic — the first American-born conductor to lead a major American orchestra. He composed the musical West Side Story (1957), a modern retelling of Romeo and Juliet set in New York. His televised Young People's Concerts introduced classical music to millions of children.
Theatre
Antoinette Perry
Known as 'Tony', she was an actress, director and producer, and one of the first women to direct on Broadway. She co-founded the American Theatre Wing, which organised entertainment for troops during WWII. The Tony Awards — the highest honour in American theatre — were established in 1947, the year after her death, and named in her honour.
Art
Grant Wood
American regionalist painter from Iowa, best known for American Gothic (1930). The two figures are his sister Nan Wood Graham and his dentist Dr Byron McKeeby — not a married couple, as often assumed. The painting is now at the Art Institute of Chicago. Wood believed art should celebrate ordinary American rural life.
Art
Edward Hopper
American realist painter whose work captures urban loneliness and the light of everyday American life. His most famous painting, Nighthawks (1942), shows four figures in a late-night diner in New York City and has become one of the most recognisable images in American art. He also painted empty hotel rooms, petrol stations and New England lighthouses.
Art
James Whistler
Born in Massachusetts but spent most of his life in London and Paris. His painting Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1 (1871) — known as Whistler's Mother — is now in the Musée d'Orsay in Paris. Whistler championed 'art for art's sake' and famously sued the critic John Ruskin for libel in 1878, winning one farthing in damages.
Literature
P. G. Wodehouse
Full name Sir Pelham Grenville Wodehouse. Created the much-loved comic duo Bertie Wooster, a cheerfully clueless English aristocrat, and Jeeves, his supremely competent valet. The first Jeeves story appeared in 1915. Wodehouse wrote over 90 books and was knighted just weeks before his death in 1975 at the age of 93.
