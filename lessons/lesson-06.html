Task 1 — Three Families of Idioms

🧠 Body, animals and DOWN.

Read each idiom and its example aloud.

🖐️BODY
arms, legs, noses, thumbs — your body describes money, jokes and clumsiness
  • hit the nail on the head
    to say exactly the right thing; to describe a problem perfectly
    You hit the nail on the head — we lose because we never practise.
  • cost an arm and a leg
    to be very expensive
    Those new trainers cost an arm and a leg.
  • get out of hand
    to become impossible to control
    The party got out of hand when fifty strangers turned up.
  • pull someone’s leg
    to joke with someone by telling them something untrue
    Relax, I’m pulling your leg — the test is next week.
  • break a leg!
    good luck! (said before a performance)
    “It’s my first concert tonight.” — “Break a leg!”
  • be all thumbs
    to be clumsy with your hands
    I’m all thumbs when it comes to sewing.
  • pay through the nose
    to pay far too much for something
    We paid through the nose for tickets at the door.
  • get cold feet
    to become nervous and change your mind just before something important
    He got cold feet and didn’t go on stage.
  • see eye to eye
    to agree with someone
    My sister and I don’t see eye to eye about music.
🐾ANIMALS
animals behave badly — secrets escape, searches fail, the weather goes wild
  • it’s raining cats and dogs
    it’s raining very heavily
    Take an umbrella — it’s raining cats and dogs.
  • the elephant in the room
    an obvious problem that everyone avoids talking about
    His bad marks were the elephant in the room at dinner.
  • let the cat out of the bag
    to tell a secret by accident
    Mia let the cat out of the bag about the surprise party.
  • a wild goose chase
    a long, hopeless search for something you will never find
    Looking for that shop was a wild goose chase — it closed years ago.
  • a lame duck
    a person or organisation that is weak and can’t manage without help; a leader whose time in office is ending
    The club has become a lame duck since its best players left.
  • the black sheep
    the member of a family who is different or causes trouble — a colour idiom too!
    My uncle is the black sheep of the family.
  • when pigs fly
    never — it will never happen
    “Will he tidy his room?” — “Yes, when pigs fly.”
⬇️DOWN
down = sad, wasted — or down to the basics
  • be down in the dumps
    to feel sad and low
    She’s been down in the dumps since her team lost.
  • go down the drain
    to be wasted; to be lost completely
    All that work went down the drain when my laptop crashed.
  • get down to brass tacks
    to start talking about the most important, practical facts
    Enough chatting — let’s get down to brass tacks.
  • down to earth
    practical, sensible and friendly; not proud
    For a famous actor, she’s really down to earth.
  • let someone down
    to disappoint someone by not doing what they hoped
    I promised to help, and I won’t let you down.

Exercise 1 — Reported Speech: Statements

🔍 Look at the sentences and answer the questions below.

1a

She said: ‘I’m on the lookout for something to read.’

1b

She said that she was on the lookout for something to read.

2a

He told the journalist: ‘It was the challenge of writing a story that could be read between two stations.’

2b

He told the journalist that it had been the challenge of writing a story that could be read between two stations.

3a

She said: ‘I’ll try this story on my teenage kids.’

3b

She said she would try that story on her teenage kids.

a

How do tenses usually change when we go from direct to reported speech?

b

What do you think could, would, should, might, the past perfect, infinitives and gerunds all have in common when they go from direct to reported speech?

c

Do you think tenses change when the reporting verb is in the present or present perfect (e.g. He says, He has said)?

d

Apart from the tense, what words change when we put sentence 3a into reported speech?

e

What is the difference between say and tell?

f

Do we always need to use that after say or tell?

🔑 Answers

a They usually go one step back into the past: present simple → past simple, present continuous → past continuous, past simple / present perfect → past perfect, will → would, can → could. I’m on the lookout → she was on the lookout.

b They don’t change. There is nowhere further back for them to go.

c No. James says he loves listening to hip-hop. — the situation is still true now, so the tense stays.

d The pronouns and possessives (I → she, my → her) and this → that.

e tell needs a person straight after it: He told the journalist that… say doesn’t: She said that… (or said to me).

f No — that is optional. She said (that) she would try that story.

Exercise 5 — Direct → Reported

🔁 Put the sentences in direct speech into reported speech.

Finish each sentence. that is optional, and you can leave out the full stop.

1

Angela told

Show answer

2

‘I’m going to start writing a short story next week.’

Show answer

3

‘The crowd went mad when I played this song last night.’

Mikey said

Show answer

4

‘I might see you tomorrow but I’ll tell you tonight, Ella.’

Dan told

Show answer
Dan told Ella (that) he might see her the next day but he would tell her that night.
5

‘I love listening to hip-hop records.’

James says

Show answer
James says (that) he loves listening to hip-hop records. — present reporting verb, so no change!

Jenny told

Show answer
Jenny told Luke (that) they had to finish that before they could leave.
