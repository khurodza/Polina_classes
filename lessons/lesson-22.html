🔎 Why · the marker decides the form
  • 1 exhibited: their time is over, the paragraph is about the past → past simple.
  • 2 would become: establishing what ___: at that moment it was still in the future → would + base (the future in the past). Became is accepted too.
  • 3 its: a possessive before a noun: its name. It’s = it is.
  • 4 runs: the river still runs through the region today → present simple. The official key gives runs (the Indus runs); run, agreeing with rivers, is accepted too.
  • 5 The largest: a superlative needs the, and the sentence starts with it.
  • 6 headed: from 1902 to 1931: a finished period → past simple.
  • 7 unearthing: a comma and the same subject (he oversaw digs and unearthed seals) → an -ing clause.
  • 8 are made: seals don’t make themselves → passive; they are still made of soapstone → present.
  • 9 has been achieved: to date = up to now → present perfect; by scholars → passive.
  • 10 used: the farmers of the Vedic era, in the past → past simple.

1
What you see when you approach Stonehenge depends on how you look. A quick glance shows a circle of rather tall, greyish, chunky stones, some of which have beams called “lintels” across the top linking two neighbours - like a doorway, or an “H” with the middle line across the top. Look more carefully and you will see similar stones but fallen on the ground. These large pillars are all sarsen – a form of stone made from grains of sand that have hardened and bonded to form a tough kind of sandstone. Once you identify the two kinds of stone, you may begin to notice the care and craft that went into building Stonehenge. The builders worked with stone as if it were wood.
2
When Stonehenge was in use, the entire outer ring of sarsen beams was connected by lintels. These crossing stones were held in place with a mortise and tenon joint. On the bottom of the lintel, a mortise (the carved-out portion) fits over the standing sarsen’s tenon (the part sticking out of the top). This is similar to the way wooden beams were attached in homes of that time and place. The major difference is that each beam that was raised at Stonehenge weighed 35 tons and could hold up a castle or a cathedral.
3
Since Stonehenge is located on a vast plain, it’s clear that the sarsen beams were brought here for somewhere else. In order to determine where the stones came from, scientists analysed the stone beams and identified them as spotted dolerite. It can be found only in the Preseli Hills of southwest Wales, which means that the stones were brought, or moved by natural forces, approximately 150 miles to the site in Stonehenge. Historians are still unable to figure out how this remarkable feat was accomplished. Since there are no written accounts of the period or remains of the materials that could have been used to move such massive stones over the vast distance, it is unlikely that the answer to this conundrum will ever be found.
4
While the origin of the stones that make up Stonehenge remains unknown, the purpose of the structure has been agreed upon for over 300 years. In the 1720s, William Stukeley popularised the idea that Stonehenge was a prehistoric temple. He also assumed that the priests who used the site were Druids - people similar to the religious leaders whom Julius Caesar saw when Rome invaded Britain. This theory echoed the conclusions drawn by 17-th century scholar and England’s first professional archaeologist John Aubrey. However, neither Stukeley nor Aubrey could have known that Stonehenge was built more than 2,000 years before the time of the Druids. This discovery was made in the 2010s, thus proving the most common understanding of Stonehenge to be false.
5
What makes the study of Stonehenge and its origins even trickier is the lack of any remains showing that it was used by people. Luckily, there is quite a large area surrounding Stonehenge, including such sites are Woodhenge and the Southern Circle that resemble Stonehenge in one way or another. The site known as Woodhenge is comprised of concentric circles of timber. There are also markings left behind by rotted wooden pillars that could have been totem poles or pillars that were holding up a roof. The Southern Circle looks a lot like Woodhenge, and archaeologists suspect that it could have been the centre of a large settlement that included up to 1,000 houses. In this case, the wooden structures look like a building plan for northern Europe’s largest Neolithic village. While this doesn’t exactly answer the question of Stonehenge’s purpose, this discovery gives scientists a new direction to look into.

  • 2 → E: the joint is similar to the way wooden beams were attached in homes (familiar), but each beam weighed 35 tons (a grand scale).
  • 3 → D: nobody can explain how the stones were moved, and it is unlikely that the answer will ever be found.
  • 4 → G: the Druid-temple idea, held for 300 years, proved false in the 2010s.
  • 5 → B: Woodhenge and the Southern Circle resemble Stonehenge and give a new direction. A is the trap: look into sounds like “further excavations”, but no digging is asked for.

Let us begin with the scenario. You are standing next to a six-year-old boy in Pormpuraaw, an isolated Aboriginal community in Australia. Why is it that a six-year-old boy in one culture can do something effortlessly that educated experienced adults in another culture struggle to do? The surprising answer, as Lera Boroditsky reported in the Wall Street Journal in 2010, might be the substantial influence of our native language over our thought processes. I will be using some of her ideas to help me make my points.

The relationship between language and thought has provided a rich topic for anthropological, psychological, and linguistic research. Language, like culture, is a system that we all create and define together, but unlike culture it is tightly structured.2 False

The idea that language might shape thought has often been seen as crazy and irrational and untestable at best. However, a new flurry of cognitive scientific research has caused a shift in consensus and led us to wonder: Do the languages we speak have an effect on the way in which we construct our individual perceptions of reality and how we see the world?

Consider a snippet from one of the most well-known nursery rhymes in the English-speaking world: “Humpty Dumpty sat on the wall”. From this one line alone, we can reveal so many of the differences between the languages. If you were singing “Humpty Dumpty” in English, you would have to mark the verb for tense and say “sat” rather than “sit” to indicate timing. But in Indonesian you don’t need to, and in fact, you can’t, change the verb to mark tense. In Russian the word that you would use would reveal the gender of your protagonist. So your verb would be slightly different if it was Mr Dumpty than Mrs Dumpty doing the sitting.3 True

Lastly, in Turkish, you would have to include in your verb how you obtained the information that you were talking about. So you would use a slightly different word if you saw Humpty Dumpty sitting with your own eyes than if you read or heard about it.

So, as we can see, each language requires very different things from its speakers. But does this mean that speakers of English, Turkish, Russian, and Indonesian end up comprehending their experiences differently just because they speak different languages?

Let’s do another one. Suppose that I want to tell you that I saw Uncle Mike on 47th street. In Mayan, which is a language spoken in Papua New Guinea, the verb that I would see would reveal whether the event happened just now, yesterday or a longer time ago, whereas in Indonesian it wouldn’t even reveal if it was still going on. In Russian the verb would reveal my gender. And in Mandarin Chinese, I would have to specify in my word choice for “uncle” whether my uncle is maternal or paternal, and whether he is related by blood or by marriage because there are different types of words for all these types of uncles.

But while the rules of all those languages seem to add specific information to the content of my sentences, some languages actually remove information altogether. In Piraha, which is a language spoken in the Amazon by a tribe of the same name, I couldn’t say “47th” because the Piraha have no words for numbers.4 Not Stated: only the number, not the whole story

In fact, in this way and many others, Piraha departs from what were long thought to be essential features for all languages.

As Rafaela von Bredow reported in Der Spiegel, the Piraha use only three pronouns. They hardly use any words associated with time or colour, and past verb conjugations don’t exist. But what has perplexed linguists the most is that the Piraha appear to have no words for numbers. They don’t use words such as “few” or “many”. There is one word “hoi” which comes close to the numeral 1. However “hoi” can also be used to describe relatively small quantities such as two small fish as opposed to one big fish.5 False

Peter Gordon, a psycholinguist at Columbia University and Daniel Everett from Bentley University visited the Pirhanas attempting to test their mathematical abilities. They were asked to repeat patterns made between 1 and 10 or to remember whether Gordon had placed 5 or 8 objects in a can. The results were fascinating; the Piraha simply could not grasp the concept of numbers.
